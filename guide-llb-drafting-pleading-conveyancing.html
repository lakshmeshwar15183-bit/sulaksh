LLB Sem 8: Drafting, Pleading and Conveyancing (CLP-1)

CLP-1 is the practical heart of LLB Sem 8 — plaints, written statements, bail pleas, writs, deeds and wills. Unit-wise plan inside.

← All Guides

Drafting, Pleading and Conveyancing — paper code CLP-1 — is the most practical paper of LLB Semester 8. After seven semesters of learning what the law is, this paper asks whether you can use it: can you turn a client's story into a plaint that survives Order VII Rule 11, write a bail application that actually gets bail, or draft a sale deed that does not collapse over stamp duty? The DU syllabus for this paper (Faculty of Law, five-year integrated B.A.LL.B. (Hons) / B.B.A.LL.B. (Hons), Semester VIII) is built around that single promise — converting facts into legally sustainable documents — and the examiner marks you like a senior checking a junior's draft: precision first, flair later.

Paper snapshot — the seven units

The syllabus moves in drafting order: Unit I lays foundations (nature, scope and importance of drafting, pleadings versus conveyancing); Unit II covers civil pleadings under the Code of Civil Procedure — plaints, written statements, interlocutory applications, execution petitions and summary suits; Unit III turns to matrimonial and succession pleadings under personal laws and the Indian Succession Act; Unit IV handles constitutional pleadings — writ petitions and Special Leave Petitions with grounds, maintainability and reliefs; Unit V covers criminal pleadings — bail applications, complaints under special statutes and applications under the BNSS; the remaining units take up conveyancing and commercial instruments (deeds, agreements, powers of attorney, wills) and close with revision, integration and professional ethics. Notice the arc: private disputes, family, Constitution, crime, property, ethics. Learn it in that order and every later unit reuses the discipline of the earlier ones.

Unit I — think like a drafter, not an essayist

Most students lose marks here before writing a single draft, because they answer drafting questions like theory answers. Unit I wants three distinctions in your head: pleadings (what you file in court — plaint, written statement), conveyancing (what transfers rights outside court — deeds, wills), and drafting (the skill covering both). The examiner's favourite trap is asking for the purpose of rules you treat as decoration — why does Order VI insist on material facts and not evidence? Because pleadings define the battlefield; evidence is the ammunition brought later. One line of purpose per rule is worth more than a page of paraphrase. Keep a one-page sheet: pleading vs conveyancing, substantive vs procedural law, and the golden vocabulary — prayer, verification, valuation, limitation, jurisdiction — with one-line meanings. You will reuse this sheet in every unit.

Unit II — civil pleadings are a format game (CPC)

This is the heaviest unit and the most scoring, because it is almost entirely format + mandatory ingredients. For a plaint, memorise the skeleton: title, cause title, valuation and court fee, cause of action with dates, jurisdiction, limitation, relief, verification. For a written statement: traverse every allegation (deny specifically — evasive denial is admission), add additional pleas (limitation, set-off, counter-claim). The examiner typically gives a fact scenario — a dishonoured cheque, a breach of agreement — and asks you to draft the plaint or spot defects in one. Practice rule: draft one plaint and one written statement per week from newspaper disputes, and always end by checking jurisdiction, limitation, court fees and stamping — the syllabus names these four expressly as things a competent drafter must address, and they are the four cheapest marks in the paper. Interlocutory applications (Order XXXIX injunctions), execution petitions and summary suits (Order XXXVII) follow the same skeleton with one twist each — learn the twist, not a new format.

Units III and IV — family sensitivity, constitutional precision

Matrimonial and succession pleadings test a different muscle: statutory compliance plus human sensitivity. Petitions under Hindu marriage law, Muslim law of marriage and divorce, guardianship, and succession under the Indian Succession Act all turn on which forum, which ground, which relief. Students mix up grounds across statutes — make a comparative table (statute → petition → ground → forum → limitation) and this unit becomes memory, not confusion. Constitutional pleadings then flip the skill: writ petitions under Articles 32/226 and SLPs demand grounds, maintainability and reliefs stated with surgical clarity. The examiner checks whether you know what makes a writ maintainable (fundamental rights for Article 32, any legal right for 226, alternative remedy, delay and laches) before rewarding your drafting. A writ without a maintainability paragraph is a failing writ — write that sentence first in practice, and it will come first in the exam too.

Unit V — criminal pleadings and the liberty lens

Bail applications dominate this unit, and they are marked through the liberty lens the syllabus stresses: courts ask about flight risk, tampering, gravity, and parity. A good bail draft states facts briefly, cites the correct BNSS provision, addresses each bail factor expressly, and prays specifically (regular, interim, or anticipatory — with the correct section). Complaints under special statutes test whether you plead the ingredients of the offence rather than narrating a story. Drill: take one bail fact-pattern and draft it three ways — regular, anticipatory, cancellation of bail — so the differences in grounds and prayer become muscle memory. Applications under the BNSS (then CrPC) reward provision-number accuracy; keep a running list of sections you actually use in drafts rather than memorising the code index.

Conveyancing — where deals die over details

Sale deeds, mortgage deeds, lease deeds, partnership agreements, powers of attorney and wills look like templates, and that is exactly why students underprepare them. The examiner's game is the defective deed: a sale deed without a habendum clause, a will without attestation, a power of attorney that is not registered where registration is compulsory. Learn each instrument as recitals → operative clauses → schedules → execution/attestation, then learn the one fatal defect per instrument. Registration, stamp duty and attestation requirements are pure memory marks — a table of instrument → stamp → registration → attestation carries this whole section. Wills deserve special care: capacity, sound disposing mind, attestation by two witnesses, and the difference between privileged and unprivileged wills under succession law.

How to write answers here

Use the Fact → Provision → Draft method. State the given facts in two lines, name the applicable provision or Order, then draft in proper format with headings — never as a running essay. Examiners award step marks for format complied with: title, numbering, verification, prayer. Where the question asks to critique a draft, list defects provision-wise (wrong forum, barred by limitation, no verification) and redraft only the defective portion — that shows senior-level judgment. Close every long answer with the four-fold check the syllabus emphasises: jurisdiction, limitation, court fees/stamping, registration. Even as a closing line it signals professional standards, which the final unit on ethics explicitly rewards.

Scoring template: For any "draft the plaint/application" question — (1) correct title and forum, (2) dated cause of action, (3) jurisdiction + limitation stated, (4) numbered paragraphs with verification, (5) precise prayer. Those five elements are ~70% of the marks before the examiner even reads your content.

Four-week revision plan

Week 1: Unit I vocabulary sheet + two plaints + two written statements from fact patterns. Week 2: matrimonial table + one writ petition + one SLP skeleton + bail trio (regular/anticipatory/cancellation). Week 3: conveyancing — one deed per day with the fatal-defect table, plus wills. Week 4: mixed previous-year-style scenarios under timed conditions, each ending with the jurisdiction–limitation–fees–registration check. The official course-structure PDF sits in LLB (Hons) → Semester 1 → Syllabus on Sulaksh — keep it open while revising so your formats track the exact units DU prescribed.

Wills, powers of attorney, and the attestation trap

Two instruments deserve their own revision block because they generate the paper's most mechanical — and therefore most losable — marks. A will requires testamentary capacity, a sound disposing mind free of coercion, and attestation by two competent witnesses who see the testator sign; privileged wills (soldiers, mariners in active service) relax formality, and the examiner tests whether you know exactly which relaxations apply where. Revocation (later will, burning, marriage in some contexts), probate versus letters of administration, and succession certificates for debts complete the testamentary cluster — prepare it as a capacity → formality → revocation → probate chain. A power of attorney turns on a different axis: general versus special authority, and the registration question that decides validity for immovable-property transactions. The recurring exam fact-pattern authorises an agent to sell land through an unregistered POA — the answer is that the authority to transfer immovable property requires registration, and an unregistered document fails at the threshold regardless of its drafting quality. Pair wills and POAs on one comparative page (purpose, capacity, attestation/registration, revocation) and this corner of conveyancing becomes four guaranteed marks rather than four feared ones.

Jurisdiction, limitation, court fees: the four-fold check

The syllabus names jurisdiction, limitation, court fees, stamping and registration as the drafter's standing duties — treat them as a closing ritual for every draft you practise. Jurisdiction means pecuniary (does the claim value fit the forum), territorial (where the cause arose or the defendant resides), and subject-matter (civil court vs tribunal vs writ forum) — plead all three in one line. Limitation means computing the period from the correct starting date and pleading condonation where delay exists; a brilliant plaint filed a day late is a dead plaint, and examiners plant limitation defects deliberately. Court fees and valuation follow the relief claimed — ad valorem where the statute demands it, fixed fees where it permits — and stamping/registration decide whether the underlying transaction document can even be received in evidence. In the exam, write this check as the final paragraph of every drafting answer even when not asked: "The plaint is within jurisdiction (cause arose at…), within limitation (filed on…), properly valued at ₹… with court fee of ₹…, and the agreement is duly stamped and registered." Seniors call this covering yourself; examiners call it professional standards — and the final ethics unit grades exactly that instinct.
