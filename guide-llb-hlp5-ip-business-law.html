LLB Sem 8: Honours HLP-5 — Patent Law, M&A and Business Law

HLP-5 honours cluster — patents from filing to compulsory licensing, plus mergers and acquisitions end to end. Unit-wise plan inside.

← All Guides

Honours cluster HLP-5 in LLB Semester 8 groups the business-facing honours options — led by Patent Law and Mergers & Acquisitions, alongside options the syllabus lists such as IP Law, Business Law, and Law and Technology. This guide covers the two anchor papers end to end and shows how the options plug into the same foundations. HLP papers are examined at honours depth: the examiner assumes you know the basics from earlier semesters and grades the second paragraph — the policy tension, the comparative position, the reform argument. Read every topic here twice: once for the rule, once for the fight about the rule.

Patent Law — Units I and II: what can be patented, and how

Foundations (Unit I) set up the patent bargain — twenty years of exclusivity in exchange for disclosure — and India's specific settlement of it: product patents restored in 2005, but fenced by Sections 3 and 4 exclusions, with Section 3(d)'s enhanced-efficacy bar on evergreening as the provision you must be able to explain in your sleep (Novartis is not optional reading here, it is the answer to half the policy questions). Unit II's patentability criteria — novelty, inventive step, industrial application — plus the procedural chain (filing, publication, examination, pre- and post-grant opposition, grant, renewal) form the paper's technical core. Examiners test procedure through defects: a missed opposition window, an incomplete specification, a wrong priority claim. Keep a procedure flowchart with timelines and fees-sensitivity, and a patentability checklist you can run on any hypothetical invention — checklist-then-conclusion is the answer shape this unit rewards.

Patent Law — Units III to V: rights, licensing, frontiers

Rights and infringement (Unit III) turn on claim construction — the patentee's monopoly is exactly as wide as the construed claims — plus direct and indirect infringement, defences (Bolar/experimental use, prior user, government use), and remedies (injunctions, damages, account of profits). The examiner's classic is the pharma dispute: evergreening attempt versus access-to-medicine defence, argued through 3(d) and compulsory licensing. Unit IV makes that fight explicit — compulsory licensing (the Natco–Bayer compulsory licence as India's landmark), voluntary licensing, patent pools, and the public-interest architecture linking patents to health emergencies. Unit V faces the frontiers: AI inventorship (the Thaler/DABUS litigation the syllabus itself cites — can a non-human be an inventor?), standard-essential patents and FRAND, biologics and data exclusivity debates. Every frontier answer should close with India's calibrated position (innovation incentives balanced with access), because that balance is the paper's thesis from Unit I to Unit V.

M&A — Units I to III: concepts, framework, deal types

Mergers & Acquisitions rewards students who learn deals as sequences, not definitions. Unit I's conceptual base (merger vs amalgamation vs acquisition vs takeover, friendly vs hostile, asset vs share deals) must be exact — the examiner distinguishes deal types by consequences (stamp duty, tax, employee transfer, shareholder approval), so define each type with its consequence. Units II–III build India's framework: Companies Act merger machinery (scheme, NCLT sanction, shareholder and creditor meetings), SEBI Takeover Code (open-offer triggers and thresholds, exemptions, competing offers), and the Competition Commission's combination review. The highest-yield preparation is trigger-based: open-offer thresholds, CCI notification thresholds, delisting triggers — a thresholds table answers numerical-adjacent questions directly. For each deal type (merger, demerger, slump sale, share purchase, hostile bid), memorise one Indian deal you can narrate in four lines; examiners in this paper grade example-backed analysis far above abstract correctness.

M&A — Units IV and V: stakeholders and practice

Unit IV distributes rights and liabilities across the deal table — shareholders (exit opportunities, valuation grievances, class rights), creditors (scheme protections, objections), employees (transfer of undertakings, compensation continuity), directors (fiduciary duties in change-of-control, disclosure), and regulators (NCLT, SEBI, CCI, RBI for cross-border legs). Answer stakeholder questions party-by-party with remedy per party; omnibus "all stakeholders are protected" paragraphs fail. Unit V is pure practice — due diligence, valuation methods, deal documentation (SPA warranties, indemnities, conditions precedent), closing mechanics, and post-merger integration disputes. This unit is examined through drafting-flavoured problems ("list the DD red flags", "draft the conditions precedent") — prepare it as checklists, not essays. Cross-border elements (FEMA pricing, overseas investment) are the contemporary layer; one cross-border structure sketch completes any deal answer.

The HLP-5 options around the anchors

Depending on your centre's offerings, HLP-5 may present options like broader IP Law (trademarks, copyright, designs as a system — prepare the "which IP for which asset" decision tree), Business Law (company and commercial-paper fundamentals at honours depth), or Law and Technology (the bridge to HLP-6's tech cluster). Strategy: anchor your preparation in Patent + M&A (this guide), then attach each option with a two-page bridge note linking it to the anchors (e.g., trademark licensing via the patent-licensing logic of Unit IV). Examiners for option papers use the same honours rubric — rule plus policy fight — so the answer method transfers wholesale.

How to write HLP-5 answers

Use the Rule → Tension → Position → Reform chain. State the governing provision or regime precisely, surface the policy tension inside it (access vs incentive in patents; efficiency vs stakeholder protection in M&A), take India's argued position with a case or deal, and close with one reform direction. Thresholds, timelines and sections must be numerically exact — honours examiners treat wrong numbers as wrong answers. For problem questions, lead with the checklist (patentability checklist; DD checklist) and let the conclusion fall out of it; the method is the mark.

Scoring template: Exact provision/threshold + landmark (Novartis / Natco–Bayer / one Indian deal) + policy tension argued both ways + one reform. Honours depth means the second paragraph — tension and position — carries more marks than the first.

Four-week revision plan

Week 1: patentability checklist + procedure flowchart + 3(d)/Novartis essay core. Week 2: infringement/remedies + compulsory licensing + frontier capsules (Thaler, SEP/FRAND). Week 3: M&A thresholds table + framework (NCLT scheme, Takeover Code, CCI) + one Indian deal per type. Week 4: stakeholder remedies + practice checklists + timed honours-style answers with the four-step chain. Thresholds and timelines: the numbers sheet

Honours examiners in this cluster treat wrong numbers as wrong answers, so maintain one numbers sheet and rehearse it weekly. On the patent side: the twenty-year term and its renewal discipline, opposition windows (pre-grant representation timing, post-grant opposition period), and the compulsory-licence waiting period after grant. On the M&A side: open-offer trigger thresholds and offer size, creeping-acquisition limits, delisting thresholds, CCI notification thresholds and review phases, and NCLT scheme meeting majorities. For each number, note the consequence of crossing it — a threshold without its consequence is trivia, while threshold-plus-consequence is advice, and this paper grades advice. In the exam, write numbers with their source (section, regulation) rather than bare; sourced numbers survive examiner scepticism, and one sourced threshold per answer compounds into the precision impression that defines honours first-division scripts.

Trademarks, copyright, designs: the option-paper bridge

Where your centre offers the broader IP or business options inside HLP-5, build them on a single decision-tree rather than as separate subjects: which IP protects which business asset? Patents guard technical solutions (prepared above); trademarks guard origin signals — acquire distinctiveness, survive deceptiveness and descriptiveness objections, enforce through infringement and passing-off (the unregistered owner's weapon, and a perennial exam favourite); copyright guards expression — originality's modest Indian threshold, ownership in employment and commissioned works, fair dealing's closed list of purposes, and remedies with account-of-profits arithmetic; designs guard appearance — novelty plus eye-appeal, the functionality exclusion, and the trademark-design overlap problem. The examiner's beloved question hands you a product (an app interface, a drug brand name, a fashion article) and asks for the protection strategy — answer with the tree: technical core → patent, brand layer → trademark, creative layer → copyright, look-and-feel → design, and say what each cannot cover. Licensing connects back to the patent Unit IV logic (exclusivity monetised through permission, with competition-law limits on abusive terms), and assignment formalities (writing, registration where required) mirror the conveyancing discipline from CLP-1. Two pages of decision-tree plus one product-worked example carry the entire option paper.

Competition law overlay: where IP and M&A meet the regulator

Both anchors of HLP-5 converge on one regulator, and questions increasingly exploit the junction. In patents, competition law polices abuse of dominance through IP — excessive pricing of essential drugs, SEP holders breaching FRAND commitments, patent pools as cartels in disguise — with the patent-competition interface (and which forum decides) as the doctrinal prize. In M&A, the combination regime (notification thresholds, appreciable-adverse-effect analysis, gun-jumping penalties, remedies from divestiture to behavioural conditions) is examined through failed or conditioned deals. Prepare the overlay as three ready arguments: when IP exclusivity becomes abuse (dominance + exclusionary conduct + no objective justification), when a combination harms competition (market definition → effects → efficiencies defence), and who decides what (CCI, NCLT, sectoral regulators, and their jurisdictional frictions). One abuse-of-dominance illustration and one combination illustration, each narrated in five lines, complete the cluster: with them, no HLP-5 question — anchor or option — lacks a closing competitive-effects paragraph, which is precisely the paragraph honours examiners weight most.
