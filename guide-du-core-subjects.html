DU Semester Strategy: Core, SEC, VAC, AEC & GE

A week-by-week system for preparing your semester papers — which subject to open first, how to split your calendar, and what the last ten days should look like.

← All Guides

Every DU student gets the same advice before semesters: "padh lo bas" (just study). That is not a plan. A real plan answers three questions: what do I open first, how many hours go to which paper, and what happens in the last ten days. This guide answers all three for Core, SEC, VAC, AEC and GE papers.

Step 1: Sort your papers into three buckets

Before opening any book, list every paper this semester and put each in one bucket:

  • Heavy (theory): subjects with long conceptual answers — Political Science theory, Hindi literature, Economics papers. These need the most calendar days.
  • Medium (semi-technical): subjects with a mix of concepts and fixed formats, like Quantitative Methods or Financial Accounting basics.
  • Light (scoring): SEC/VAC-style papers where the syllabus is compact and questions repeat in pattern. These give the best marks-per-hour return.

The common mistake is starting with whatever subject your friend is studying. Start with one Heavy and one Light paper in parallel — the Light paper keeps your momentum up while the Heavy one grinds forward slowly.

Step 2: The 40–35–25 week split

Take the number of weeks you actually have before exams (not the weeks on the academic calendar — the real ones after assignments and festivals).

  1. First 40%: first full reading. Your goal here is not memorising; it is marking. Highlight topics that appear in both your syllabus and past year papers. Those double-marked topics are your exam.
  2. Next 35%: second reading of only the marked material, plus writing practice — one full answer per study session, handwritten, timed.
  3. Final 25%: PYQs under exam conditions and revision from your short notes only. No new topics in this phase, no exceptions.

Step 3: Use the syllabus as a weapon

The official syllabus tells you the unit weightage. Many DU papers draw heavily from specific units. Open your syllabus, and next to each unit write how many questions came from it in the last three years. Study units top-down by that count. This single exercise routinely cuts 30–40% of useless reading.

Practical tip: Keep the official syllabus PDF and at least three years of question papers open side by side while planning. On Sulaksh these live inside each Core Subject → pick semester → 📗 Syllabus / 📄 PYQs.

The last 10 days

In the final stretch, stop learning and start recalling. Take each marked topic and write its skeleton from memory: headings, sub-points, one example. Wherever the skeleton collapses, revise just that topic. Do not re-read entire chapters — re-readers walk into exams knowing everything except what gets asked.

Common mistakes worth avoiding

  • Collecting notes from five sources for every topic. One good source finished beats five half-done.
  • Ignoring internal assessment/assignment marks. They are the cheapest marks of the semester.
  • Studying only what you enjoy. Exams do not ask your favourite unit exclusively.

Semesters reward systems over moods. Build the buckets, follow the split, and let the syllabus decide your effort — that is the whole method.

FAQ

How many hours should a DU student study daily during semester time? Two focused hours on weekdays and four to five on weekends is enough if you follow the bucket system — consistency across weeks beats occasional marathon days.

Are SEC and VAC papers easy marks? They are compact, not effortless. Because the syllabus is small, competition per mark is high and examiners expect precise answers. Prepare them fully, but do not assume they need zero effort.

Should I study from standard publications or college notes? Use your teacher's notes as the base because exams are set from taught material, then fill gaps from one standard source. More than two sources per subject wastes reading time.
