LLB Sem 8: Criminology and Victimology (OP-3)

Why crime happens, how punishment works, and where victims stand — schools of thought to victim rights. Unit-wise plan inside.

← All Guides

By Sulaksh Editorial Team · Written from the official DU Faculty of Law Sem VIII syllabus (EC 1282) · Reviewed Sept 2026

Every criminal trial pretends the only question is did he do it. Criminology and Victimology — optional paper OP-3 — spends five units insisting on two harder questions: why did it happen, and who looked after the person it happened to? The first question embarrasses statute-memorisers, because no section answers it. The second embarrasses everyone, because for most of legal history the answer was "nobody". DU's Sem VIII syllabus walks both questions in order — causation, classification, punishment, victim theory, victim rights — and the students who top this paper are the ones who argue with it out loud while preparing. So read this guide noisily. Take sides. The examiner will reward you for having had the fight beforehand.

Round one: five schools walk into a crime

Set them arguing and Unit I memorises itself. The classicist opens: people choose, so punish swiftly and certainly and they will choose otherwise. The positivist scoffs: choice is shaped — biology, psychology, circumstance — treat causes, not just choices (and handle Lombroso's wilder claims the way the syllabus intends: historically important, scientifically outgrown). The sociologist blames the room, not the person: strain between goals and means, behaviour learned in company, labels that manufacture the deviance they claim to describe. The economist shrugs: crime is opportunity arithmetic; move the incentives, move the rate. Your answer is the referee's verdict, and the verdict is never a clean win — strain theory fits property crime's link with inequality beautifully and explains almost nothing about white-collar fraud; labelling fits policed neighbourhoods and little else. One school, fairly stated, fairly criticised, with one Indian illustration: five such paragraphs, one revision page, half the paper handled.

Round two: name the beast before prescribing the cure

Unit II's typologies exist for one reason — different crimes need different control strategies, and prescribing without diagnosing is quackery. White-collar crime hides behind respectability and spreadsheets: answer with audits, regulators, disclosure — never with more beat constables. Organised crime runs on enterprise logic and corrupted access: answer with conspiracy law and special statutes. Cybercrime scales infinitely and respects no border: answer with intermediary regimes and digital-evidence procedure. Gendered and hate crimes wound communities, not just individuals: answer with recognition plus protection, not generic policing. When the examiner asks why ordinary methods fail against a type, point at the type's structure. And attach one Indian example per type — a scam, a syndicate prosecution, a breach, a hate-crime trial — because typology without cases is stamp-collecting, and this paper grades hunters, not collectors.

The punishment question India never quite settles

Deterrence, retribution, prevention, reformation, restoration — recite all five and then do the brave thing: say what India actually does, which is a constitutional blend leaning reformative beneath deterrent rhetoric. Bachan Singh gates retribution behind rarest-of-rare; Sunil Batra carries rights into the prison; Rama Murthy sketches the reform agenda undertrial overcrowding keeps defeating. Around the theories sits the machinery — sentencing discretion, plea bargaining's cautious Indian career, probation and parole, open prisons — each learnable as provision, purpose, and one ground reality that embarrasses the purpose. The BNSS's community-service and speedy-trial push is your bridge to the present: cite it to show the machine moving, and name the direction honestly. Examiners can smell memorised theory paragraphs; they lean forward for the sentence that says what is true.

The second half: victims enter the courtroom

Here is where ranks are decided, because most candidates arrive with empty hands. Unit IV gives you the vocabulary of the wound the system itself inflicts: precipitation (interaction explained, never blame assigned — fumble this distinction and the answer curdles), vulnerability as a property of situations rather than persons (age, gender, poverty, custody), and secondary victimisation — delay, hostility, exposure — the trial as second assault. Unit V converts vocabulary into instruments: compensation funds and NALSA/DLSA schemes, the victim's lawyer and appeal against acquittal, witness protection, in-camera trials, identity suppression, and restorative experiments from mediation to Lok Adalats. Every instrument gets the same four-beat treatment: the need it answers, the provision that creates it, the gap that starves it (money, awareness, delay), the reform that would feed it. Need, instrument, gap, reform — write victim answers in that heartbeat and the examiner hears competence.

The notebook habit that lifts this paper

One credible figure per answer: prison occupancy against capacity, undertrial share, sessions conviction rates, compensation actually disbursed, reporting rates for gendered crime. Figures function here the way case citations function elsewhere — proof you read beyond the textbook. Track three sources through the semester (NCRB prison numbers, NALSA disbursal reports, a Law Commission report or two) and never invent a statistic; an approximate-but-honest framing keeps faith, a fabricated decimal destroys it. And keep moralising out — neither criminals as monsters nor victims as saints. The syllabus tests analytic empathy. Preachiness is what students write when analysis runs dry; make sure yours never does.

A fifteen-marker, built live

Question: "Mob justice in the age of viral videos — analyse through criminological theory and victimology, and suggest responses." Watch the construction. Open with strain plus labelling in one breath: forwarded outrage as anomie (norms suspended in the crowd), labelling as the accelerant (the "criminal" tag applied before trial, sometimes before police). Second paragraph, typology: collective violence as its own type — diffusion of responsibility, deindividuation — which is why conventional deterrence aimed at individuals misfires; the matched responses are platform-level (virality friction, takedown speed) and procedural (fast-track investigation, witness protection when the mob turns on witnesses). Third, penology: retribution satisfies the street but the Constitution demands reformative process — cite the fair-trial and prison-rights lines. Fourth, victims: the lynched person's family faces secondary victimisation from footage circulation itself — compensation schemes plus takedown-plus-prosecution of circulation. Close with reform in resourcing terms (who funds the fast track, who staffs victim support). Five paragraphs, each from a different unit, one contemporary phenomenon. That is not five mini-answers stapled together; it is one argument walking through the syllabus in order. Examiners describe such answers to each other in the staff room. Be that answer.

The one-page war room

Consolidate the paper onto a single page you can reproduce from memory the night before. Five schools in five lines (founder, claim, criticism). Four crime types with matched responses (white-collar–audit, organised–special statutes, cyber–intermediary regimes, gendered–recognition-plus-protection). Three punishment cases (Bachan Singh for gated retribution, Sunil Batra for prison rights, Rama Murthy for reform). Four victim instruments with gaps (compensation–disbursal delays, counsel-and-appeal–awareness gaps, witness protection–funding gaps, restorative pilots–scale gaps). Three figures (occupancy, undertrial share, one disbursal number). That is the whole paper in about forty lines. If any line goes blank in rehearsal, that blank is your next study session — the war room doubles as a diagnostic. Students who carry this page into the final week stop studying everything and start repairing specific holes, which is the only efficient way to spend the last seven days. And in the hall, before opening the question paper, reproduce the page roughly on the last sheet of the answer book — not to copy from (that would be malpractice), but because the act of reproduction steadies recall the way a musician's scales steady the fingers. Preparation you can perform is preparation you own.

The BNSS bridge: new codes, same victims

India's new criminal codes did not just renumber old provisions; they tilted procedure toward the victim, and this paper expects you to know where. Witness protection finds statutory footing, victim compensation timelines tighten, the victim's right to be heard at bail and sentencing stages expands, and zero-FIR plus e-FIR mechanics lower the reporting threshold that secondary victimisation once kept high. None of this is self-executing — that sentence, with one implementation gap attached (protection funding, compensation disbursal delays, police training), is the difference between describing the BNSS and analysing it. Prepare a two-column sheet (old Code position versus BNSS position) for every victim-relevant provision you meet, and deploy the comparison whenever a question mentions reform, pendency, or access. Examiners in 2026 read "CrPC" where "BNSS" belongs as a dating signal; make sure your answers carry the current year. The codes changed; show that you changed with them, and the contemporary marks follow.

Usual doubts

Can I skip the schools and just do victims? You could pass, but you would surrender the paper's easiest marks. Five schools × five lines (founder, claim, strength, criticism, Indian example) is a single page. There is no cheaper page in Sem 8.

Theory or data — what does the examiner want? Theory argued, data deployed. A school debated plus one figure plus one instrument-with-gap is a complete fifteen-marker. That is the whole paper in one sentence.
