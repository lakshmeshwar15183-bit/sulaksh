LLB Sem 8: Criminology and Victimology (OP-3)

Why crime happens, how punishment works, and where victims stand — schools of thought to victim rights. Unit-wise plan inside.

← All Guides

Criminology and Victimology — optional paper OP-3 in LLB Semester 8 — asks the two questions criminal law usually skips: why do people commit crimes, and who speaks for the victim once the state takes over the case? The DU syllabus (Faculty of Law, five-year integrated programme) splits the paper cleanly across five units — crime causation, crime classification, punishment systems, victimology theory, and victim rights — and the examiner's design is visible in that split: the first half tests whether you can think beyond statutes, the second whether you know how India is slowly rebuilding its justice system around victims, from compensation schemes to the BNSS's victim-centric provisions. Rote learners survive the first half; only readers of the second half top.

Paper snapshot — the five units

Unit I lays criminology's foundations — classical, positivist, sociological and economic schools of crime causation; Unit II classifies crime — typologies (violent, property, white-collar, organised, cyber, gendered) and what each type demands from the system; Unit III studies penology and the criminal justice machinery — theories of punishment, sentencing, prisons, reform; Unit IV introduces victimology as a discipline — victim precipitation, vulnerability, secondary victimisation; Unit V closes with victim rights and contemporary challenges — compensation, participation, protection, restorative justice. The paper's internal logic is cause → category → consequence → the forgotten party → repairing the forgetting. Write answers that respect that flow and the examiner follows you willingly.

Unit I — schools of thought are answer machines

Every causation question is really asking you to stage a debate between schools and referee it. The classical school (free will, deterrence through certain and swift punishment), the positivists (biology and psychology — Lombroso to modern biosocial work, handle with critical care), the sociologists (strain, differential association, labelling — crime as a product of structure and meaning), and the economic lens (rational choice, opportunity). The examiner's trap is one-school answers: "poverty causes crime" stated absolutely fails, while "strain theory explains property crime's correlation with inequality, but labelling theory better explains why policed communities show higher recorded crime" passes with distinction. Prepare each school as founder → core claim → one strength → one criticism → one Indian illustration. Five schools times five lines is one revision page that answers half the paper.

Unit II — typology decides the response

Classification is not taxonomy for its own sake — each crime type in this unit points to a different control strategy, and that link is what gets marked. White-collar crime (low visibility, high damage, respectability as camouflage) demands regulatory and audit responses; organised crime (enterprise logic, corruption nexus) demands conspiracy and special statutes like MCOCA-style frameworks; cybercrime (scale, anonymity, jurisdiction collapse) demands intermediary and digital-evidence regimes; gendered and hate crimes demand recognition that the harm includes community terror, not just individual injury. The examiner typically asks why conventional policing fails a given type — answer with the type's structural features, not with general complaints about police. One Indian example per type (a scam, a syndicate crackdown, a data-breach case, a hate-crime prosecution) turns abstract typology into Sem-8 answers.

Unit III — punishment theories meet Indian prisons

Deterrent, retributive, preventive, reformative, and restorative theories must be more than a list — the examiner asks which theory India's system actually embodies, and the honest answer (a constitutional blend leaning reformative, with deterrent rhetoric) needs case support: Bachan Singh's rarest-of-rare gated retribution, Sunil Batra's prison-rights jurisprudence, Rama Murthy's reform agenda. Sentencing discretion, plea bargaining, probation and parole, open prisons, and undertrial overcrowding are the institutional half of the unit — prepare each as provision → purpose → one ground reality. The BNSS's community-service and speedy-trial thrust is your contemporary bridge: cite it to show the punishment system moving, however slowly, from retribution toward restoration.

Units IV and V — victimology: the paper's scoring half

Most students arrive at victimology with empty hands, which is precisely why it decides ranks. Unit IV's core concepts — victim precipitation (handle carefully: explaining interaction is not blaming), vulnerability (age, gender, poverty, institutional custody), and secondary victimisation (the system itself wounding the victim through delay, hostility, or exposure) — each map directly onto exam questions about why victims don't report or why trials retraumatise. Unit V is applied: compensation schemes (Section 357A CrPC lineage, NALSA/DLSA schemes, victim compensation funds), participation rights (victim's counsel, right to appeal against acquittal), protection (witness protection scheme, in-camera trials, identity suppression), and restorative justice (mediation, plea-adjacent settlement, community repair). Structure every victim-rights answer as need (secondary victimisation) → instrument (scheme/provision) → gap (funding, awareness, delay) → reform — need-instrument-gap-reform is the paragraph order examiners in this unit visibly reward.

How to write answers here

Use the School → Type → System → Victim chain that mirrors the syllabus. Ground causation in named schools with criticism (never monocausal claims), tie crime types to matched responses, evaluate punishment against constitutional cases, and close victim questions with concrete instruments plus honest gaps. Data points — prison occupancy rates, conviction rates, compensation disbursal figures — function like case law in this paper: one credible figure per answer signals a student who reads beyond textbooks. Avoid moralising about criminals or victims; the paper tests analytic empathy, and preachiness reads as the absence of analysis.

Scoring template: One school with its critic + one crime type with its matched response + one punishment case (Bachan Singh / Sunil Batra) + one victim instrument (compensation / witness protection) with its gap. Four elements, each one line deep, is a complete 15-marker in this optional.

Four-week revision plan

Week 1: five-schools sheet (founder → claim → strength → criticism → Indian example). Week 2: typology table (type → structural feature → matched response → Indian case). Week 3: punishment theories + prison-reform cases + BNSS updates. Week 4: victimology concepts + instruments-and-gaps sheet + timed answers using the four-element template. The official course-structure PDF is in LLB (Hons) → Semester 1 → Syllabus on Sulaksh — check your revision against its exact five units so nothing DU prescribed slips through.

Figures that function like case law

In this paper one credible number outweighs a paragraph of adjectives, so build a data sheet and update it before the exam: prison occupancy versus capacity (the overcrowding fact behind every reform answer), undertrial share of prison population (the speedy-trial argument in one figure), conviction rates in sessions trials (the investigation-quality debate), compensation disbursal under victim schemes (the gap between promise and payment), and reporting rates for gendered crimes (the secondary-victimisation evidence). Deploy exactly one figure per answer, tied to the point it proves — occupancy for reformative-theory answers, disbursal for compensation answers, reporting for victimology answers. Never invent statistics: a wrong figure detected by the examiner discredits the whole script, while an approximate-but-honest framing ("NCRB data consistently shows…") keeps credibility intact. Sources to track are few — NCRB prison statistics, NALSA disbursal reports, Law Commission reports on sentencing and victim compensation — and one cited source per figure completes the academic apparatus this paper quietly grades.

Vulnerable victims: women, children, custodial settings

Victimology becomes concrete — and examination-grade — when it differentiates vulnerability, because the system's duties multiply with the victim's powerlessness. Gendered victimisation spans domestic violence (protection-officer architecture, residence orders, monetary relief), sexual offences (consent jurisprudence, procedural safeguards from FIR to trial, compensation), and workplace harassment (committee duties, employer liability) — each regime answers a different failure, and the examiner checks whether you match victim to instrument instead of reciting schemes generically. Child victims trigger the POCSO universe: child-friendly recording, special courts, mandatory reporting, and the tension between protection and the rights of juvenile accused — prepare this as a safeguards-list answer, since questions here are almost always "evaluate the protective framework." Custodial vulnerability (prisoners, institutionalised persons, encounter contexts) invokes constitutional tort, NHRC guidelines, and magisterial oversight. The analytic thread across all three: vulnerability is not a trait of the victim but a property of the situation the system created or tolerates — write that sentence once per answer and the rest of the paragraph organises itself around state duty rather than victim sympathy.

Restorative justice and the reform horizon

The paper's closing move asks whether punishment-centred justice can absorb repair — and this is where original thinking earns the most disproportionate rewards, because few students prepare it. Restorative models (victim-offender mediation, plea-adjacent settlement with victim voice, community service as repair, Lok Adalat disposal of compoundable matters) must be presented with both promise and limits: repair without coercion, and the non-negotiable boundary that serious offences cannot be mediated away. Plea bargaining's Indian career (limited scope, judicial cautions, victim-compensation linkage) illustrates the system's ambivalence about negotiated justice — use it as your balanced case study. Then close with the reform horizon the examiner expects Sem-8 students to see: witness-protection funding and enforcement, compensation disbursal delays, trauma-informed policing and prosecution, and BNSS-era victim-participation provisions given real institutional teeth. Structure reform answers as diagnosis (which secondary victimisation?) → instrument (existing or proposed) → resourcing (who pays, who staffs) → metric (how we know it worked). Resourcing-and-metric is the sentence most answers omit and the sentence that signals a future lawyer rather than a present student.
