LLB Sem 8: Air and Space Law (OP-3)

From Chicago Convention to Artemis Accords — air liability, aviation security and space treaties. Unit-wise plan inside.

← All Guides

By Sulaksh Editorial Team · Written from the official DU Faculty of Law Sem VIII syllabus (EC 1282) · Reviewed Sept 2026

In December 1903 two bicycle mechanics stayed airborne for twelve seconds, and within forty years the world needed an entirely new branch of law to manage what those twelve seconds unleashed. In 1957 a beeping metal sphere the size of a beach ball did the same thing for outer space. Air and Space Law — optional paper OP-3 in LLB Sem 8 — is the story of law sprinting to catch up with machines, told in six units across DU's syllabus. Students pick it thinking it will be light. It isn't light; it is young, which is better — young law means the principles are still visible, unburied by centuries of exceptions. Learn the story straight and the paper tells itself.

1944: the sky gets a constitution

Every answer in the air-law half begins in Chicago, 1944, with one blunt sentence: each state owns the sky above its territory — complete, exclusive sovereignty. International aviation exists only because states then trade pieces of that sovereignty through the freedoms of the air: overflight, technical stops, and the traffic rights that let an airline actually carry your neighbour to another country. Get the first five freedoms straight and you own Unit I, because each freedom is a real bargain your examiner can smell. Then meet the referee: ICAO, which sets global standards through its Annexes but does not directly run any airline — a distinction students fudge and examiners punish. India's layer is DGCA licensing, tightly negotiated bilaterals, and UDAN carrying connectivity to towns that never saw a runway. Sovereignty, traded freedoms, a standards-setter, an Indian regulator: that four-beat rhythm opens nearly every air-law answer you will write.

The strictest liability in transport (and its numbers)

Here the romance ends and the arithmetic begins — which is why this unit tops students who prepare it. The Warsaw system's low gold-franc limits grew, crash by crash and treaty by treaty, into the Montreal Convention's two tiers: strict carrier liability up to roughly 128,821 SDRs, presumed fault beyond it. Delay, baggage, cargo: three heads, three limit regimes, one exclusive-remedy doctrine. The examiner will hand you a delayed flight, a lost suitcase, a grieving family — and mark whether you named the Convention, placed the claim in its tier, applied the limit, and remembered the defences (contributory negligence; wilful misconduct under older Warsaw). Make the SDR table once and rehearse one fact-pattern per head. And keep the Indian twist ready: passengers who sue in consumer fora alongside Montreal, because in India the two tracks genuinely collide. One paragraph on that collision, and a competent answer becomes a distinguished one.

Hijackings, unruly flyers, and who owns the route

Security law is history with statutes attached: each convention answers a wave of incidents — Tokyo, Hague, Montreal on hijacking and sabotage, later protocols on unruly passengers — so always pair the instrument with the incident class it was built for. India's anti-hijacking framework is your domestic anchor. Then the money half: mergers reviewed, slots fought over, fares investigated, passengers charmed with charters of rights. A merger clearance here, a DGCA penalty there — collect two current capsules (one security, one economic) and rotate them through your answers. Examiners in optionals do not expect scholarship; they expect awareness, and awareness has a date on it.

Upwards: five principles against the infinite

Space law looks vast until you realise the Outer Space Treaty gave it five load-bearing walls: space as the province of all mankind, no national appropriation, exclusively peaceful use, state responsibility for national activities, and liability for damage. Unit VI's entire contemporary gallery — debris fields, mega-constellations, lunar mining plans, military shadows — is just those walls under stress, one issue per wall. Debris tests responsibility. Mining tests non-appropriation. Constellations test mankind's province and harmful interference. Militarisation tests peaceful use. Learn the treaty set around them (Liability, Registration, the Moon Agreement and precisely why it failed), add India's authorisation window through IN-SPACe and the national-legislation push, and finish with the comparison examiners cannot resist asking: space liability is fault-based where Montreal is tiered-strict. Prepare that contrast as a table and half the space-law paper is pre-written before you enter the hall.

Sitting the paper: habits of an internationalist

Name every treaty with its year — wrong years sink credibility faster in this paper than anywhere else in Sem 8. Compute liability answers instead of discussing them; bounds and tiers beat adjectives. And when space-law memory thins, reason openly by analogy from the air-law solution and say you are doing so — structured analogy is real legal method, not padding, and saying it aloud shows the examiner you know the difference. Close each answer with one live flashpoint: debris, constellations, mining, Artemis, drones, a merger. The syllabus ends in the present tense; your answers should too.

Before the exam, a checklist

Freedoms of the air, recitable in order. ICAO-versus-DGCA roles, one line each. Montreal tiers and SDR figures, exact. Three conventions with their incident classes. The five OST principles, each paired with its stressed contemporary issue. IN-SPACe and the space-legislation position. One air-sovereignty case, one liability decision, Cosmos 954 for space liability practice. Tick these off against DU's course-structure PDF (LLB (Hons) → Semester 1 → Syllabus on Sulaksh) and walk in knowing the sky — both skies — has been covered.

The freedoms, once and for all

Students always ask for the freedoms list "in exam form", so here it is, with the memory hooks that keep it ordered. First freedom: overfly without landing (transit — the sky as a road). Second: land for technical reasons — fuel, repairs — without picking up traffic (the garage stop). Third: carry traffic from your home state to another (outbound). Fourth: carry traffic to your home state from another (inbound). Fifth: carry traffic between two foreign states as part of a route touching home (beyond rights — the airline's prized possession). Sixth through ninth (combinations, cabotage, pure foreign carriage) matter less for DU's questions but know they exist so a question mentioning "cabotage" does not ambush you — cabotage is domestic carriage reserved for national carriers, the sovereignty principle at its purest. The examiner's favourite twist is asking which freedoms need specific bilateral grant versus which flow from multilateral transit agreements — first and second come cheap (transit agreements), third onward are bargained bilaterally, which is precisely why bilaterals are the currency of aviation diplomacy. Draw the five as a ladder once, annotate each rung with one Indian bilateral reality (a Gulf route, a Southeast Asian one), and Unit I is done forever.

A liability problem, solved aloud

Try one with me. Facts: Delhi–London flight, six-hour delay, checked-in bag lost, passenger demands ₹5 lakh for the bag and ₹2 lakh for the delay. Step one: name the regime — Montreal Convention, as India is a party and this is international carriage. Step two: split the heads — baggage is Article 17(2)/22 territory with its own limit (around 1,288 SDRs, roughly ₹1.4 lakh at typical rates — compute, don't guess wildly); delay is Article 19/22(1) with a separate limit (around 5,346 SDRs), and the carrier escapes delay liability only by proving all reasonable measures were taken. Step three: defences and exclusives — contributory negligence reduces, wilful-misconduct uplift belongs to old Warsaw and does not apply here, and Montreal is the exclusive remedy so no parallel-Convention shopping. Conclusion: bag claim capped at the baggage limit, delay claim within its limit subject to the reasonable-measures defence. Four paragraphs, each doing one job. That is a fifteen-marker in this unit, and every liability question in the paper is this shape wearing different facts.

The treaty-year drill card

Chicago 1944. Tokyo 1963, Hague 1970, Montreal 1971 on security. Warsaw 1929 to Montreal 1999 on liability. Outer Space Treaty 1967, Rescue 1968, Liability 1972, Registration 1975, Moon 1979. Recite them like a pilot's checklist, because in this paper a wrong year is not a small error — it suggests the whole convention that follows is approximate. Pair each year with one line of content (what the treaty does), and drill both directions: year-to-content and content-to-year. Ten minutes a day for a fortnight fixes them permanently, and permanent is what you need, since these dates also anchor your contemporaneity — debris debates reference 1972 liability, drone debates reference 1944 sovereignty. Old treaties, new problems: that sentence, with exact years attached, is the voice of this paper.

Students ask

Is this paper really easier than the other optional? Not easier — cleaner. Young regimes mean fewer exceptions to memorise, but treaty precision (names, years, figures) is graded mercilessly. If you like stories with numbers inside, it is yours.

Air or space — where do marks come from? Liability (Montreal tiers) on the air side; principles-under-stress on the space side. The air–space liability comparison question is the paper's gift — prepare it as a table.
