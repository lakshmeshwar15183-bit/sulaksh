LLB Sem 8: Air and Space Law (OP-3)

From Chicago Convention to Artemis Accords — air liability, aviation security and space treaties. Unit-wise plan inside.

← All Guides

Air and Space Law — optional paper OP-3 in LLB Semester 8 — is the paper students pick for love and top with discipline. It covers two of the youngest legal regimes on earth: the law of the skies, barely a century old, and the law of outer space, barely sixty. The DU syllabus (Faculty of Law, five-year integrated programme) runs them as one continuous story across six units, and that is exactly how the examiner thinks — air law's solutions (sovereignty balanced with transit rights, strict carrier liability, security conventions) are the templates space law copies, adapts, or breaks. Learn the parallels and you effectively prepare two papers' worth of answers with one set of ideas.

Paper snapshot — the six units

Unit I introduces air law — Chicago Conference, sovereignty over airspace, the freedoms of the air; Unit II builds the international civil aviation regime — ICAO, bilateral air service agreements, India's DGCA framework; Unit III tackles liability — Warsaw/Montreal Convention systems, carrier liability for death, injury, delay and baggage; Unit IV covers aviation security and economic regulation — hijacking conventions, unruly passengers, competition and consumer protection; Unit V opens space law — Outer Space Treaty principles, the common heritage idea, national space legislation including India's newer framework; Unit VI closes with space treaties on liability and registration plus contemporary issues — mega-constellations, debris, resource mining, militarisation. Units I–IV and V–VI mirror each other (regime → liability → security/contemporary), so revise them as mirrored pairs.

Units I and II — sovereignty versus the freedom to fly

The founding tension of air law is one sentence: every state has complete sovereignty over its airspace (Chicago Convention, 1944), yet international aviation exists only because states trade transit rights. The freedoms of the air — especially the first five (transit, technical stop, and traffic rights) — are the examiner's favourite list because each freedom maps to a real bilateral bargain. India's position (restrictive bilaterals, DGCA licensing, UDAN's domestic connectivity push) gives you the national layer every answer needs. Structure answers as principle (sovereignty) → exception (freedoms by agreement) → institution (ICAO standards) → India (DGCA/bilaterals). ICAO's role is often misunderstood: it sets standards (Annexes), it does not directly regulate airlines — saying that precisely, with one example like safety audits, earns the clarity marks most scripts miss.

Unit III — liability: the strictest regime in transport law

Air carrier liability is where this paper turns numerical and therefore scoring. The Warsaw-to-Montreal evolution — from low gold-franc limits to the Montreal Convention's two-tier system (strict liability up to ~128,821 SDRs, presumed fault beyond) — is the backbone; delay, baggage and cargo rules plus exclusive-remedy questions complete it. Examiners love computation-adjacent problems: a delayed flight, lost baggage, a death claim — identify the Convention, the tier, the limit, the defences (contributory negligence, wilful misconduct under older Warsaw). Keep an SDR-limits table with the three liability heads (passenger, baggage, cargo) and rehearse one fact-pattern per head. Consumer-forum overlap (Indian passengers suing under consumer protection law alongside Montreal) is the contemporary twist that converts a good answer into a great one.

Unit IV — security and the business of flying

Aviation security law reads like a response to history: Tokyo, Hague and Montreal conventions on hijacking and sabotage, unruly-passenger protocols, and India's anti-hijacking framework. Pair each instrument with the incident class it answered — examiners reward that causal link. The unit's second half is economic: airline competition, mergers, slot allocation, and passenger rights (refunds, denied boarding, DGCA's passenger charter). This is where non-law reading pays: one current example — a merger review, a fare-war complaint, a DGCA penalty — placed inside a doctrinal answer signals Sem-8 maturity. Prepare two "current affairs capsules" (one security, one economic) and rotate them across answers.

Units V and VI — space: principles first, problems second

Space law answers fail when students list treaties without the five Outer Space Treaty principles that organise everything: province of all mankind, non-appropriation, peaceful use, responsibility of states for national activities, and liability for damage. Every contemporary issue in Unit VI hangs on one principle under stress — debris on responsibility, mining on non-appropriation, mega-constellations on province-of-mankind and harmful interference, militarisation on peaceful use. The Liability and Registration Conventions plus the Moon Agreement (and why it failed) complete the treaty set; India's space activities bill/framework is your national layer. The examiner's signature question contrasts air and space liability (fault-based in space vs the Montreal tiers in air) — prepare that comparison as a ready-made table and half the paper is pre-written.

How to write answers here

Use the Treaty → Principle → India → Contemporary chain. Name the governing convention, state its core principle in one line, add India's statute or institutional position, and close with one live issue (debris, drones, UDAN, Artemis). Treaty names and years must be exact — wrong years destroy credibility in this paper faster than anywhere else. For liability problems, always compute or bound the answer (tiers, limits, defences) rather than discussing liability abstractly. And exploit the air–space mirror: when short on space-law material, reason by analogy from the air-law solution and say explicitly that you are doing so — structured analogy is legitimate legal method, not padding.

Scoring template: Convention + year → one-line principle → India's position (DGCA/ISRO/framework) → one contemporary flashpoint (debris, mining, unruly flyers, merger). Treaty precision + one live example is the 12+/15 formula in this optional.

Four-week revision plan

Week 1: freedoms-of-the-air list + ICAO/DGCA sheet + sovereignty paragraph. Week 2: liability tables (Montreal tiers, baggage, cargo) with one problem per head. Week 3: security conventions + two current-affairs capsules. Week 4: OST five principles + air-vs-space liability comparison + contemporary issues (debris, constellations, mining, Artemis). The official course-structure PDF is in LLB (Hons) → Semester 1 → Syllabus on Sulaksh — revise against its exact unit order so nothing DU prescribed goes uncovered.

Exam-day tactics for an international-law paper

Treaty papers punish two opposite errors — vague generality and drowning detail — so ration your specifics deliberately. Open every long answer with the governing instrument named with its year; that single habit separates prepared scripts from bluffing ones within the first line. Spend your detail budget on one worked illustration per answer (a liability computation, a principle-under-stress analysis) rather than sprinkling half-remembered protocol names across the page. When a question touches a treaty you barely recall, answer from its parent principle (sovereignty, strict liability, province of mankind) and reason downward — principled derivation stated openly earns partial credit with dignity, while invented specifics earn zero and damage the rest of the script's credibility. Attempt the liability problem compulsorily: it is the paper's only quasi-numerical question, it has objectively right steps, and step marks there are the cheapest fifteen marks in the optional. Finally, keep fifteen minutes to re-check every treaty year and SDR figure you wrote — in this paper, a corrected year is a rescued answer.

Drones, UDAN, and private space: India's contemporary layer

Every answer in this paper improves with an Indian contemporary paragraph, so build one reusable capsule. On the air side: the Drone Rules framework (categorisation, no-permission-no-takeoff zones, the digital-sky platform idea) shows sovereignty logic applied to unmanned flight — the same Chicago-Convention sovereignty from Unit I, miniaturised to 120 metres. UDAN demonstrates the state using aviation as connectivity policy (viability-gap funding, unserved routes), which answers any "aviation and development" question. Airline-insolvency episodes (a carrier collapsing mid-ticket-sale) connect Units III and IV in one example: passenger refunds under the passenger charter, lessor repossession fights, and DGCA's consumer-protection role. On the space side: IN-SPACe as the authorisation and licensing window for private space activity, the push toward a comprehensive national space legislation, and India's landing and station ambitions as context for Artemis-era cooperation debates. The examiner does not expect technical depth on any of these — one accurate paragraph showing that treaty principles now operate through Indian institutions is precisely the national layer the marking scheme rewards. Maintain this capsule as a living page and refresh it the week before the exam.

Case-bank: the awards and disputes to carry in

International-law papers are won on citation discipline, so fix a small, high-quality case-bank rather than spraying treaty names. Carry one case per theme: an air-sovereignty or transit dispute illustrating bilateral bargaining, a Montreal Convention liability decision showing the two-tier system working (exclusive remedy and limits both visible), an aviation-security prosecution or convention application demonstrating universal jurisdiction logic, a space-liability precedent (Cosmos 954's claims practice remains the classic illustration of the Liability Convention in action), and one boundary or resource dispute (geostationary-orbit claims, debris-removal debates) showing principles under stress. For each, memorise exactly three things: forum, holding in one line, principle illustrated. In answers, deploy cases as the third step of the chain (treaty → principle → case → India → contemporary) rather than clustering them in a list — distributed citation reads as mastery, while a case-dump paragraph reads as panic. Five cases, three facts each, fifteen facts total: that is the entire memorisation burden of this paper's international half.
