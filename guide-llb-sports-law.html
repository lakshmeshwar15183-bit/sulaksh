LLB Sem 8: Sports Law (OP-4)

Sports governance, contracts, doping, betting and esports — the business-meets-law optional. Unit-wise plan inside.

← All Guides

By Sulaksh Editorial Team · Written from the official DU Faculty of Law Sem VIII syllabus (EC 1282) · Reviewed Sept 2026

You already know more sports law than you think. You have shouted about a transfer that "can't be legal", fumed when a fixing scandal got a two-year ban, and argued whether a sprinter's testosterone case was fair or cruel. Sports Law — optional paper OP-4 — simply asks you to convert those living-room verdicts into legal reasoning. DU's five-unit Sem VIII syllabus is arranged like a season: who governs the game, who gets rich from it, who keeps it honest, and what is changing right now. Bring your fandom. Leave your commentary voice at the door. What follows is how one becomes the other.

First whistle: who is actually in charge?

Every governance answer starts from a paradox your examiner adores: federations insist they are private clubs — their rules, their tribunals, their autonomy — while spending public money, picking national teams, and answering Parliament's questions. India lives this paradox loudly: the Lodha overhaul of cricket administration (tenure caps, cooling-off, one-state-one-vote, professional management), recognition norms for national federations, courts stepping into botched elections. Argue both sides like you mean them — autonomy protects sporting expertise and global uniformity; accountability protects athletes and public funds — then land. Add one CAS award you genuinely understand (an eligibility fight, a transfer war, a doping appeal) and the answer stops being an essay about sport and becomes sports law. Without the Indian controversy and the international award, it is just an opinion with citations missing.

The money game: rights, faces, and fine print

If governance is politics, commercialisation is arithmetic — which makes it the most reliably scoring unit. Broadcasting rights turn eyeballs into property (exclusivity deals, must-share-listed-events fights, the telecast-rights litigation every Indian student should know). Sponsorship breeds its shadow, ambush marketing: the official partner pays crores while the clever intruder steals the association for lakhs — courts have drawn that line more than once, and each judgment is an exam answer waiting to happen. Image rights ask who owns a player's face when the athlete, the club, and the federation all claim it — India's fastest-moving frontier in this paper. And player contracts are contract law back in spikes: transfers, retention systems, restraint of trade against reasonable protection, injury and termination clauses. One Indian commercial dispute per topic, collected like trading cards through the semester. In this unit, example density is analysis.

The integrity file: dope, fix, fraud

Three threats, three different legal designs — mixing them up is how good students lose this unit. Doping runs on strict liability: the substance in the body is the offence, with specified substances, therapeutic-use exemptions, and sanction gradations as the only escape routes. Know WADA's architecture, NADA's Indian enforcement, and one sanctioned athlete's case with its reasoning — not its headlines. Fixing splits in two the moment you look closely: sporting fraud (corrupting the contest itself) versus gambling regulation (skill against chance, a State-List maze, offshore sites a click away). Conflating them is the unit's signature error. Age fraud and child-athlete safeguarding close the file with bans, verification regimes, and protection duties. For each threat, write the same four lines — threat, regime, enforcer, gap — and let the gap do the heavy lifting: testing capacity, the betting-law vacuum, whistleblower protection. Gaps are where top-band marks hide.

Extra time: esports, welfare, gender, mega-events

The final unit is a gallery that repaints itself yearly, so the examiner checks dates, not definitions. Esports: recognised sport or entertainment product, contracts without federations, betting-adjacent skin economies. Welfare: harassment mechanisms inside federations, mental-health conversations, life after the last contract. Gender eligibility: testosterone rules and their courtroom challenges — prepare this as a balanced two-sided paragraph, because a rant in either direction fails a law paper by definition. Mega-events: hosting guarantees, tax holidays, clean zones against ambush marketers, stadiums that become white elephants. Keep a running page, three lines per controversy, refreshed till exam week. One deployed controversy per answer is your contemporary layer; without it the script reads last year's.

The athlete's contract: read the fine print

Underneath the glamour sits the unit most students skip and examiners love: the player as worker. Term, pay, image assignment, conduct clauses, injury cover, termination, forum for disputes — run every contract problem through that checklist and the planted defect (an unreasonable post-term restraint, a missing forum clause, an image-rights grab) jumps out by itself. Section 27's restraint doctrine, read with employment reality, decides transfer fights. And the worker's lens — minimum standards, maternity, harassment redressal, short-career insecurity — connects this paper to labour law in exactly the interdisciplinary move honours marking rewards. Doctrine never goes stale; when headline disputes desert your memory in the hall, the contract checklist will still be there.

The transfer saga: a restraint problem solved

Take the classic: a 19-year-old academy product signs a five-year deal with a modest release clause; at 21, after a breakout season, a rival triggers it and the club sues to block the move, citing training investment and a two-year post-term non-compete. Solve it the way the examiner wants. First, the restraint test: restraints during the term protecting legitimate interests are generally enforceable; post-term non-competes restraining lawful profession face Section 27 headwinds and survive only if narrowly reasonable — two years across all Indian football for an academy graduate fails that test visibly. Second, training compensation: FIFA-style solidarity and training-reward mechanisms are the lawful way to recover investment, not servitude by contract — say which regime applies and what it yields. Third, the forum: federation tribunal, CAS on appeal, or civil court — jurisdiction decided by the contract's dispute clause and the federation's statutes, and getting the forum wrong in your answer loses the practical half of the marks. Fourth, the image-rights loose end most students drop: who owns the player's commercial face during and after the move. Four paragraphs, one dispute, four units touched. Transfer problems are restraint-plus-forum-plus-image every single time; recognise the trio and the answer is administration, not inspiration.

The season calendar: pacing five units

Five units, one semester, and every sports-law student makes the same scheduling error — spending October on governance essays and meeting doping for the first time in December. Flip it. Governance needs slow marination (the autonomy debate improves each time you revisit it), so start it early but in short sessions. Commercialisation rewards concentrated bursts: one week, four disputes, done — example density is built fast and decays slowly. Integrity regimes need spaced repetition because strict-liability architectures blur together; interleave them with contract checklists so the two steepest learning curves never coincide. Contemporary capsules are harvested, not studied: fifteen minutes every Sunday through the term, three lines per controversy, and the gallery curates itself. The final fortnight belongs to dispute shapes — one transfer problem, one doping problem, one commercial clash, timed — because by then knowledge is complete and only recognition speed matters. Pacing is strategy, and in an optional paper strategy is points.

The election that goes to court

No fact pattern recurs in this paper like the disputed federation election — rival factions, rival voter lists, a court-appointed observer, and an international federation threatening suspension for "government interference". Solve it as the governance unit's master problem. First, the autonomy claim: sporting bodies run their own affairs under their statutes and international affiliation — state it fairly, because half the marks sit in showing you understand what is being overridden. Second, the accountability triggers: public money, national representation, athlete rights, and documented malpractice — each independently capable of justifying oversight. Third, the Indian legal hooks: recognition norms, the sports-bill debate's proposed regulator and tribunal, and the courts' actual record of appointing administrators. Fourth, the landing: elections under supervised process with a roadmap back to normalcy, not permanent administration. Close with the suspension-risk paragraph — international bodies do suspend non-compliant members, and any honest answer prices that risk instead of ignoring it. One framework, and every federation-crisis question from any year fits inside it.

From the stands

Do I need to follow sports to score? It helps the way speaking the language helps in a foreign city — useful, not mandatory. Every example in this guide works from the facts stated. But if you do follow sport, start translating now: each controversy you meet is a future answer's contemporary layer.

Which unit carries the paper? No single one — but commercialisation plus integrity together hold the most dispute-shaped questions, and dispute shapes are this paper's favourite format. Governance gives you the essay core; the other two give you the marks.
