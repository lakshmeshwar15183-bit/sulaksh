LLB Sem 8: Media Laws (OP-4)

Free speech, OTT regulation, defamation, influencer law and data protection — media law after BNSS and DPDP Act. Unit-wise plan inside.

← All Guides

Media Laws — optional paper OP-4 in LLB Semester 8 — is constitutional law with a newsroom deadline. Every unit of the DU syllabus (Faculty of Law, five-year integrated programme) sits at the collision point between Article 19(1)(a) and something else: reasonable restrictions, broadcast regulation, reputation, consumer protection, privacy, or copyright. What makes this paper distinctive in 2026 is how much of it has changed recently — new criminal codes replacing IPC-era speech offences, OTT under regulatory ambit, the DPDP Act reshaping privacy, influencer commerce professionalising advertising law. The examiner is not testing whether you memorised press law of the 1990s; the paper rewards students who argue from the current regime. Read this guide with one eye on the syllabus and one on the news.

Paper snapshot — the six units

Unit I grounds everything constitutionally — free speech, its evolution through landmark cases, and the "new" speech offences under the BNSS/BNS framework; Unit II maps the regulatory machinery — press, private broadcasting, and OTT platforms with their three-tier oversight; Unit III handles civil and criminal liability for speech — defamation in both tracks plus fact-checking duties; Unit IV turns to advertising, consumer rights and influencer law — ASCI codes grown into enforceable consumer-law duties; Unit V covers privacy, data protection and the right to be forgotten — DPDP Act meets journalism; Unit VI closes with IP and emerging tech in media — copyright, fair dealing, AI-generated content, intermediary liability. The method across units is identical: right → restriction → regulator → remedy. Memorise that chain and the paper organises itself.

Unit I — speech, restrictions, and what's actually new

Foundations here mean three things examiners check ruthlessly: the scope of 19(1)(a) (expression through any medium, including digital — build from Romesh Thappar and Indian Express through Shreya Singhal to Anuradha Bhasin), the exhaustive eight of 19(2) (restrictions must fit a listed head — no free-floating "public interest" limits), and the new-code mapping (sedition's replacement, hate-speech and obscenity provisions in the BNS, procedural speech powers in the BNSS). The classic failing answer discusses Section 124A as live law; the passing answer states its replacement and what changed. Keep a then-vs-now table (IPC provision → BNS/BNSS successor → substantive difference) — it answers a third of Unit I questions by itself. Prior restraint versus subsequent punishment is the unit's other evergreen: censorship boards and takedown powers tested against the presumption against pre-censorship, with film certification and platform blocking orders as the two live illustrations.

Unit II — who regulates whom: press, broadcast, OTT

Regulatory questions are mapping questions, and maps are drawn to be reproduced. Print runs on press registration plus Press Council norms (advisory, ethics-driven); private broadcasting runs on licensing, programme and advertising codes, and content-regulation disputes; OTT and digital news sit under the IT Rules' three-tier system (platform grievance redressal, self-regulatory body, government oversight) — the most litigated media-regulation design of the decade. The examiner's signature move is comparison: press freedom with light-touch councils versus broadcast licensing versus platform self-regulation with state backstop. Answer with a regulator table (medium → entry control → content code → grievance path → leading case) and then argue which model best survives Article 19 scrutiny. One pending or recent challenge to the IT Rules' oversight tier, stated accurately, is the contemporary mark in this unit.

Unit III — defamation in two tracks plus fact-check duties

Defamation is where students bleed marks by merging two different laws. Criminal defamation (now under the BNS) requires the ten exceptions — truth for public good, fair comment, good-faith reporting — to be pleaded as defences, with punishment as the shadow behind settlement dynamics. Civil defamation is damages shaped by defences (truth, honest opinion, privilege — absolute for Parliament and courts, qualified for fair reportage). Media-specific amplifiers — republication liability, editor liability, SLAPP suits against journalists — complete the picture. The examiner gives a report and asks "liable?" — work it as statement → defamatory meaning → defences (which track?) → remedy, and state which track you are in before analysing. Fact-checking duties (platform obligations, election-period restrictions, court directions on misinformation) are the unit's modern tail — link them to intermediary due-diligence rather than treating them as press ethics.

Units IV and V — the consumer and the private self

Advertising law has quietly transformed from ASCI self-regulation into consumer-law liability: misleading ads, dark patterns, and surrogate advertising now carry Central Consumer Protection Authority consequences, and celebrity endorsers face due-diligence duties. Influencer law — disclosure norms, deceptive-practice liability, platform-specific rules — is the examiner's gift to prepared students because it has no settled textbook chapter; one disclosure-rules summary plus one enforcement example answers it fully. Unit V then turns inward: privacy (Puttaswamy's proportionality test as the master key), the DPDP Act's consent-notice-breach architecture and its journalism-adjacent exemptions, and the right to be forgotten (de-listing petitions, platform compliance, the free-speech tension courts are still calibrating). Privacy answers should always run the Puttaswamy test — legality, necessity, proportionality — on the facts; examiners in this unit visibly tick those three words.

Unit VI — copyright, AI content, intermediaries

The closing unit is the paper's future-facing scoring zone. Copyright in media (ownership of commissioned work, fair dealing's narrower Indian scope versus US fair use, music and film licensing chains) meets AI-generated content (authorship without a human author, training-data infringement suits worldwide, disclosure proposals). Intermediary liability — safe harbour conditioned on due diligence, the Shreya Singhal actual-knowledge standard versus later dilution debates, significant-intermediary obligations — underpins every platform question in the paper. Prepare this unit as three mini-essays (fair dealing in news, AI authorship, safe harbour's current scope), each ending with one pending global or Indian development. Recency is the entire game here; a 2025 development cited correctly outweighs a page of doctrine.

How to write answers here

Use the Right → Restriction → Regulator → Remedy chain in every answer: anchor in 19(1)(a) and its case evolution, fit any limit into a 19(2) head, name the regulator and its actual powers, and close with the remedy (damages, takedown, prosecution, correction). Never cite repealed provisions as live law — the then-vs-now table prevents the paper's commonest fatal error. For regulation questions, compare across media rather than describing one medium in isolation; comparison demonstrates the constitutional judgment being tested. And date your contemporary examples — "2025 IT Rules challenge" beats "a recent case" because precision is credibility in media law.

Scoring template: 19(1)(a) anchor + one landmark case + current-regime provision (BNS/DPDP/IT Rules, exact name) + regulator with powers + one 2024–26 development. Current-regime accuracy is graded harder here than in any other Sem 8 paper.

Four-week revision plan

Week 1: speech foundations — 19(1)(a) case line + 19(2) heads + IPC-to-BNS mapping table. Week 2: regulator map (press/broadcast/OTT) with grievance paths. Week 3: defamation two-track drills + advertising/influencer disclosure rules + Puttaswamy-proportionality practice. Week 4: copyright/AI/intermediary mini-essays + timed answers with the four-step chain. The official course-structure PDF is in LLB (Hons) → Semester 1 → Syllabus on Sulaksh — verify every regulator and provision against its exact six units.

Contempt, legislatures, and the media-trial problem

Two topics sit at the edge of the syllabus and the centre of the examiner's imagination. Contempt of court and reportage — scandalising, prejudicing pending proceedings, disobedience — is tested through the fair-criticism versus interference line: bona fide commentary on judgments after delivery is protected, while publications creating a real risk of prejudice during trial are not. The media-trial phenomenon (parallel adjudication on air, leaked chargesheets, character assassination before verdict) gives the examiner ready-made problems: resolve them by separating the outlet's speech right from the accused's fair-trial right, then applying the prejudice test rather than moralising about sensationalism. Parliamentary privilege versus reporting (Articles 105/194, truthful reporting of proceedings as qualified privilege, expunction and publication of expunged material) completes the institutional triangle with contempt. Prepare the triangle as legislature–court–press with one flashpoint each (privilege motion over reporting, contempt over a trial-coverage programme, expunction republication dispute), and any institutional-clash question becomes a solved pattern rather than a surprise.

Examination drills: the recurring problem shapes

Media-law problems recycle a small set of shapes — rehearse each until the answer is reflex. Shape 1: the allegedly defamatory report — run statement → meaning → track (civil/criminal) → defences → remedy, and always state the track first. Shape 2: the takedown or blocking order — test legality (statutory source), necessity (least-restrictive means), and proportionality, then check procedural safeguards (notice, hearing, review). Shape 3: the sting or hidden-camera exposé — weigh public-interest justification against privacy intrusion and entrapment concerns, with the Puttaswamy test as referee. Shape 4: the influencer endorsement complaint — apply disclosure duties and endorser due diligence, then compute liability (advertiser vs platform vs influencer). Shape 5: the de-listing/right-to-be-forgotten petition — balance rehabilitation and privacy against public interest in the information, noting the jurisprudence is still settling. For every shape, the first sentence should classify (which track, which test, which regulator) because classification is visibly the first tick on the marking scheme. Practise one problem per shape per week in the final month, each in under twenty minutes — speed at classification is the skill being built, and it transfers directly to the exam hall.
