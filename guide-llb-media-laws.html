LLB Sem 8: Media Laws (OP-4)

Free speech, OTT regulation, defamation, influencer law and data protection — media law after BNSS and DPDP Act. Unit-wise plan inside.

← All Guides

By Sulaksh Editorial Team · Written from the official DU Faculty of Law Sem VIII syllabus (EC 1282) · Reviewed Sept 2026

It is 11 pm in a newsroom. A reporter files a story naming a powerful man; the desk must decide in twenty minutes whether those paragraphs are journalism or a defamation suit. Down the hall, lawyers debate whether a web series needs cuts, an influencer's undisclosed endorsement just drew a consumer-authority notice, and someone's decade-old arrest record is surfacing on search results again. Media Laws — optional paper OP-4 — is that night desk, frozen into six syllabus units. Article 19(1)(a) on one side; eight exhaustive restrictions, three regulators, and a decade of new statutes on the other. And here is what makes this paper unlike any other in Sem 8: half of its law changed recently. The examiner knows it. Prepare the current regime or prepare to fail politely.

The first question is always: which law is alive?

Before doctrines, dates. The free-speech spine runs Romesh Thappar to Indian Express to Shreya Singhal to Anuradha Bhasin — expression through any medium, restrictions fitting only inside 19(2)'s eight heads, no free-floating limits. Then the mapping exercise that decides a third of Unit I: sedition's replacement, hate-speech and obscenity provisions in the BNS, speech-related powers in the BNSS. The student who discusses Section 124A as live law has announced, in the first paragraph, that they stopped reading two years ago. Build a then-versus-now table — IPC provision, BNS/BNSS successor, what substantively changed — and pin it above your desk. Prior restraint versus subsequent punishment is the unit's other permanent resident: film certification, platform blocking orders, and the heavy presumption against pre-censorship. One accurate line on which is which, and the examiner relaxes.

Three media, three rulebooks

Regulation questions are maps, and this unit hands you three to draw from memory. Print: registration plus Press Council ethics — light touch, advisory teeth. Private broadcast: licences, programme and advertising codes, genuine content control. OTT and digital news: the IT Rules' three-tier design — platform redressal, self-regulatory body, government oversight — the most litigated media-regulation architecture of our decade. The question is never "describe one"; it is always, openly or secretly, "compare". Which model survives Article 19 scrutiny best, and why? Answer with the table (medium, entry control, content code, grievance path, leading case) and then argue — one pending challenge to the oversight tier, stated precisely, is the contemporary mark. Vagueness about which tier does what is how scripts in this unit quietly die.

Tonight's lead story: "reporter sued"

Defamation arrives as a fact pattern and splits the moment you touch it. Criminal track (now BNS): the statement, its defamatory meaning, and the ten exceptions argued as defences — truth for public good, fair comment, good-faith reportage — with punishment looming behind every settlement talk. Civil track: damages shaped by truth, honest opinion, and privilege, absolute for Parliament and courts, qualified for fair reportage. Media amplifiers — republication chains, editor liability, SLAPP suits filed to bleed journalists through process — finish the picture. Work every problem track-first: name the track, then analyse. Track-second analysis is the unit's signature error and the easiest one to fix. And at the story's edge sit fact-check duties — platform obligations, election-time restrictions, court directions on misinformation — which belong to intermediary diligence, not press ethics. Filing them in the right drawer is itself a mark.

Ad breaks and private lives

Two units, one theme: other people's money and other people's selves. Advertising quietly grew teeth — ASCI guidance hardened into consumer-authority liability for misleading ads, dark patterns, surrogate advertising, with endorsers now carrying due-diligence duties of their own. Influencer law has no settled textbook chapter, which is good news: one disclosure-rules summary plus one enforcement example answers any question fully, and the examiner cannot ask beyond what exists. Privacy is the heavier sibling: Puttaswamy's proportionality test (legality, necessity, proportionality — tick all three, visibly, every time), the DPDP Act's consent-notice-breach machinery with its journalism-adjacent exemptions, and the right to be forgotten — de-listing petitions where rehabilitation and privacy pull against the public's interest in remembering, in jurisprudence still wet from the courts. Run every privacy fact through the three-word test and the answer organises itself.

Copyright, robots, and the platform in the middle

The closing unit faces forward. Copyright in newsrooms: who owns commissioned work, how narrow Indian fair dealing is next to American fair use, how music and film licensing chains actually run. AI content: authorship without a human author, training-data infringement suits marching through foreign courts, disclosure proposals. Intermediaries underneath everything: safe harbour conditioned on diligence, Shreya Singhal's actual-knowledge standard and everything that happened to it since, significant-intermediary duties. Prepare three mini-essays — fair dealing in news, AI authorship, safe harbour today — each ending with one pending 2025 development. In this unit recency is weight: a correctly cited recent development outweighs a page of settled doctrine.

Contempt, privilege, and trial by television

Two institutional clashes round off preparation. Contempt versus reportage: fair post-delivery criticism is protected, publications risking prejudice to live proceedings are not — and media trials (chargesheets on air, verdicts before verdicts) are examined through the prejudice test, never through outrage. Parliamentary privilege versus reporting: truthful accounts of proceedings carry qualified privilege, expunged material republished is another matter. One flashpoint per corner of the triangle and institutional-clash questions become pattern recognition.

The three-tier diagram, in words

Students keep asking for the OTT/digital-news oversight design "so it can be drawn in the exam", so here is the drawable version. Tier one: the platform itself — grievance officer, fifteen-day acknowledgement-and-decision discipline, monthly compliance reporting. Tier two: the industry self-regulatory body — an appellate layer with retired judges and experts, empowered to warn, censure, apologise, reclassify. Tier three: the government's oversight mechanism — blocking directions as the last resort, with the constitutional shadow of Article 19 hanging over every order. The litigation you must know is the challenge to tier three's design — whether executive blocking with limited hearing satisfies proportionality — because the examiner's question is always the same shape: "is this tier constitutional?" Answer tier by tier, testing each against Puttaswamy's three words, and finish with where the challenge currently stands. The diagram takes ninety seconds to reproduce and answers any question with "IT Rules" in it. In a paper where students drown in regime detail, the ones carrying diagrams swim.

The five-minute triage drill

Train classification speed, because this paper's clock punishes slow starters. Take any media fact-pattern and force yourself, in five minutes flat, to write four lines: the right engaged (which facet of 19(1)(a)), the restriction's claimed head (does it fit 19(2), and if not, the analysis ends there), the regulator with its actual powers (not its imagined ones), and the remedy proportionate to the harm. Four lines, five minutes, no prose. Do one triage daily in the last month — old newspaper controversies are perfect raw material — and full answers start assembling themselves, because a fifteen-marker here is just a triage expanded with one case and one contemporary development. Students who write full practice answers weekly but never drill triage arrive knowing the law and losing the clock. The night desk does not grade elegance under deadline; it grades correct calls, fast. Practise the desk, not the diary.

Music, film, and the licensing chain

Copyright questions in this paper almost always arrive wearing entertainment facts, so learn the chain the way producers live it. A film's music involves composers, lyricists, labels, and platforms, each holding a slice — ownership splits between employer-commissioned work and independent creation, performer rights sit alongside, and streaming adds platform licences on top. Infringement problems test the idea-expression line (tunes can infringe; vibes cannot) and the substantial-taking question, while fair dealing's closed purposes decide the newsroom's own defences — reporting current events covers genuine reportage, not wholesale lifting dressed as coverage. Remedies run from injunctions (including John-Doe orders against unknown uploaders, a favourite exam topic) to damages and account of profits. Prepare one film-music hypothetical end to end — who owns what, who needs whose permission, what happens when nobody asks — and the entire copyright corner of Unit VI becomes a single worked example you already understand.

Desk notes

What is the single costliest mistake? Citing repealed law as live — Section 124A discussed as current, IPC numbers where BNS numbers belong. The then-versus-now table exists to prevent exactly this. Check it before every answer.

How do I handle regulation questions? Compare across media; never describe one medium alone. Comparison is the constitutional judgment being tested, and lone-medium answers read as notes, not arguments.
